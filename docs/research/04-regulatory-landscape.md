# 04. Regulatory landscape

This document summarises what the main aviation regulators require or
recommend on pilot fitness, fatigue and mental health, and how the system
fits alongside those requirements. It is a research summary, not legal
advice, and rules change; check the current text of any regulation before
relying on it.

## ICAO (international standards)

- **Annex 1 (Personnel Licensing)** sets medical standards for licence
  holders, including mental fitness. Class 1 medical assessment requires that
  the applicant has no established medical history or clinical diagnosis of
  a psychiatric condition likely to interfere with the safe exercise of
  licence privileges.
- **Annex 6 (Operation of Aircraft)** requires states to establish flight
  time, flight duty period and rest requirements, and allows operators to use
  a **Fatigue Risk Management System (FRMS)** as an alternative to
  prescriptive limits.
- **Doc 9966, Manual for the Oversight of Fatigue Management Approaches**
  (2nd edition, 2016) is the reference for FRMS. It defines fatigue,
  describes the science (sleep need, circadian rhythm, time awake), and
  recommends the use of subjective fatigue scales such as Samn-Perelli and
  KSS, sleep diaries, and objective measures such as actigraphy and the PVT
  in fatigue data collection.
- **Doc 8984, Manual of Civil Aviation Medicine** provides guidance to
  medical examiners on mental health assessment and encourages a supportive,
  non-punitive approach to pilots seeking help.

## FAA (United States)

- **14 CFR Part 117** (effective January 2014) sets flight and duty limits
  for Part 121 operations, introduced after the Colgan Air 3407 accident. It
  requires each crew member to affirm fitness for duty before each flight and
  prohibits operators from assigning, and pilots from accepting, a flight if
  the pilot is too fatigued to operate safely (§117.5).
- **AC 120-103A, Fatigue Risk Management Systems for Aviation Safety**
  (2013) describes how to build an FRMS, including fatigue reporting,
  data collection with subjective scales, and non-punitive reporting policy.
- **AC 120-100, Basics of Aviation Fatigue** (2010) is a plain-language
  primer on the science of fatigue for crews and operators.
- **The IM SAFE checklist** appears in the *Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical
  Knowledge* (FAA-H-8083-25) and the *Risk Management Handbook*
  (FAA-H-8083-2) as the recommended personal pre-flight self-assessment.
- **Medical certification (14 CFR Part 67)** disqualifies certain psychiatric
  conditions, and requires disclosure of mental-health conditions and
  medications. Historically this created a strong disincentive to seek care.
  The FAA has progressively allowed special issuance for pilots on certain
  SSRI antidepressants (since 2010, expanded since) and has moved to reduce
  the reporting burden for talk therapy.
- **The Pilot Fitness Aviation Rulemaking Committee** (report, November
  2015), convened after Germanwings 9525, recommended pilot support
  programmes, mental-health training for aviation medical examiners, and
  reducing the stigma around seeking help, rather than mandatory
  psychological testing.
- **HIMS (Human Intervention Motivation Study)** is the established
  industry/FAA programme for pilots with substance-use disorders and is the
  model for confidential, peer-supported return to flying.

## EASA (European Union)

- **Regulation (EU) 2018/1042**, amending the Air Operations Regulation
  (EU) 965/2012, was the EU's response to Germanwings 9525. It introduced
  for commercial air transport operators:
  - a **psychological assessment** of flight crew before they start line
    flying;
  - a **support programme** for flight crew, providing confidential, peer-led
    assistance for psychological and well-being problems, with the aim of
    self-referral and return to work; and
  - **alcohol testing** of flight and cabin crew.
  The support programme requirement became applicable in February 2021.
- **Part-MED (Regulation (EU) 1178/2011, Annex IV)** sets medical
  certification standards, including psychiatric and psychological
  requirements, and was amended to strengthen mental-health assessment by
  aero-medical examiners.
- **Flight time limitations (ORO.FTL)** and **Fatigue Risk Management**
  (ORO.FTL.120) are the EU equivalents of Part 117 and the FRMS guidance.
- The **EASA Germanwings Task Force** (2015) and subsequent Action Plan set
  the direction: support programmes, better medical assessment, and a
  European aeromedical data repository.

## Other jurisdictions (brief)

- **UK CAA** retained the EASA framework after leaving the EU and runs its own
  guidance on peer support programmes.
- **Transport Canada** has fatigue rules (CARs Part VII, amended 2018 to 2022)
  and FRMS provisions modelled on ICAO.
- **CASA (Australia)** has fatigue rules under CAO 48.1 and FRMS guidance; the
  work of Dawson, Reid and colleagues in Adelaide underpins much of the
  fatigue science used worldwide.

## The common thread

Across all regulators the direction since 2015 is the same:

1. **Fitness for duty is the pilot's personal responsibility**, affirmed
   before every flight.
2. **Fatigue is managed with data**, using subjective scales, sleep records
   and non-punitive reporting.
3. **Mental health is addressed through support, not surveillance.** The
   consistent finding is that mandatory testing drives problems underground,
   while confidential peer support brings them into the open.

## How this system fits

| Regulatory expectation | System feature |
| --- | --- |
| Pre-flight fitness self-affirmation (Part 117, ORO.FTL) | Structured daily check-in built on IM SAFE |
| FRMS subjective fatigue data (Doc 9966, AC 120-103A) | Samn-Perelli, sleep and wake questions, with trend view |
| Support programmes and self-referral (2018/1042, Pilot Fitness ARC) | Verdicts that point to peer support and the AME; never to management |
| Non-punitive, confidential handling | Local-only data, no accounts, no upload |

The system is a **personal aid** that sits upstream of these processes. It
does not replace an operator's FRMS, fitness-for-duty policy or support
programme, and it does not produce records that an operator or regulator
should rely on.
